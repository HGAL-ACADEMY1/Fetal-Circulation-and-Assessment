👋 Hello, students! Welcome to class. Today, we are going to explore something truly magical: Fetal Circulation and how we check if a baby is growing happily inside the womb.
Imagine you are a deep-sea diver. You have a tank on your back for air, right? 🤿 Well, a baby inside the womb (in utero) is a lot like that diver.
As early as the 3rd week of life inside the womb, the baby’s blood starts exchanging nutrients with the mom. But here is the big difference:
The baby does NOT use their lungs to breathe oxygen like we do! 🙅‍♂️🌬️
Instead, the baby gets all their oxygen and gets rid of carbon dioxide through the Placenta.
Does blood go to the baby's lungs? Yes, but only a tiny bit just to keep the lung cells alive. The lungs aren't doing the "breathing" job yet.
Because of this, the baby has special structures—think of them as "traffic shortcuts"—that send blood to the most important organs: the Brain 🧠, Liver, Heart ❤️, and Kidneys.
Let's follow the blood flow! 🩸
Blood comes from the placenta, super rich in oxygen. It travels to the baby through the Umbilical Vein.
Wait, a vein carrying oxygen? Yes! Usually, veins carry dirty blood, but in the fetus, the Umbilical Vein carries the good stuff towards the heart.
This blood hits the first shortcut called the Ductus Venosus.
The Ductus Venosus takes most of that rich blood, bypasses the liver (it gives the liver just a little), and dumps it straight into the Inferior Vena Cava.
From there, it goes into the Right side of the heart (Right Atrium). 🫀
Now, normally blood would go to the lungs next. But remember, the lungs are closed for business! So, the blood takes the second shortcut: the Foramen Ovale.
This is a little door between the Right Atrium and the Left Atrium. The blood slips through there, goes to the Left Ventricle, and is pumped out to the body via the Aorta! 🚀
But what about the blood coming back from the baby’s head and arms? That enters the heart and tries to go to the lungs.
It needs to be redirected away from the lungs too! So, it uses the third shortcut: the Ductus Arteriosus.
This connects the Pulmonary Artery directly to the Aorta. So the blood skips the lungs and goes down to the lower body.
Finally, the blood—now low on oxygen—needs to go back to mom to get recharged. It travels through the Umbilical Arteries back to the placenta. 🔄
Fun fact: The baby’s oxygen level is only about 80% of what ours is! To make up for this, the baby’s heart beats super fast—120 to 160 beats per minute! 💓
Okay, let’s now move on to… How we measure Growth and Development! 📏
Nurses have a huge responsibility here. We must get signed consent, explain everything to the mom (and her support person), and make sure she is comfortable physically and mentally.
If a procedure has any risk, we need "Additional Consent" so the mom knows exactly what might happen. Safety first! 🛡️
Let’s talk about estimating growth. One famous method is McDonald’s Rule. (No, not the burgers! 🍔❌)
This uses a tape measure on the mom's tummy. Between 20 and 31 weeks, the height of the uterus in centimeters usually equals the number of weeks she is pregnant!
So, 24 weeks pregnant = 24 cm belly height. Easy, right?
We measure from the pubic bone (symphysis pubis) up to the top of the uterus (fundus).
⚠️ Note: This rule stops working well in the 3rd trimester because the baby starts gaining weight rather than just getting taller.
If the measurement is too big? Could be twins! 👯‍♀️ Or too much fluid.
If it's too small? The dates might be wrong, or the baby might not be developing correctly.
We also look for milestones:
• 12 weeks: Uterus is just peeking over the pubic bone.
• 20 weeks: It’s at the belly button (umbilicus).
• 36 weeks: It’s all the way up at the ribs (xiphoid process).
Now that we understand growth, let’s talk about… Fetal Well-Being! Is the baby happy inside? 🤔
First up: Movement! ⚽
Mom usually feels the baby move (Quickening) around 18-20 weeks. A healthy baby moves a lot!
If the placenta isn't working well, the baby moves less to save energy. A healthy baby should move at least 10 times a day.
We can use the Sandovsky Method: Mom lies on her left side after a meal and counts. Ideally, she feels movements 10 to 12 times in an hour.
If she feels fewer than 5 movements in that hour? She should call her doctor immediately! ☎️
Or the Cardiff Method (Count-to-Ten): She times how long it takes to feel 10 kicks. Usually, it happens within 60 minutes.
Remember to tell mom: Babies sleep too! 😴 So sometimes they are just napping. Don't panic immediately, but stay observant.
Let’s now dive into… The Heart Rate 💓
Normal Fetal Heart Rate (FHR) is 120 - 160 bpm. We can hear this with a Doppler tool as early as 10-11 weeks.
We do something called Rhythm Strip Testing. We put a monitor on mom’s belly for 20 minutes.
We look for two things:
1. Short-term variability: Tiny second-to-second changes. This shows the baby's nerves are getting oxygen.
2. Long-term variability: Bigger changes over 20 minutes.
When the baby moves, the heart rate should go UP. Just like when you run, your heart beats faster! 🏃‍♂️
Next, we’ll look at… The Non-Stress Test (NST).
This is safe and easy. Mom pushes a button every time the baby kicks.
A Reactive Result (This is GOOD! ✅) means:
Every time the baby moves, the heart rate goes up by 15 beats for 15 seconds. We want to see this happen 2 times in 20 minutes.
A Non-Reactive Result (This needs checking ⚠️) means no heart rate increase when moving, or no movement at all.
If the baby won't move, we might give mom some orange juice 🍊 (sugar wakes the baby up!) or use Vibroacoustic Stimulation.
That’s a fancy way of saying we use a buzzer to make a sharp sound (ZZZTT! 🔊) on mom's belly to startle and wake the baby.
At this point, it’s important to consider… Contraction Stress Testing (CST).
This is a tougher test. We want to see how the baby handles the stress of a contraction (like during labor).
We used to use IV drugs to start contractions, but that was risky. Now, we use Nipple Stimulation.
When mom rubs her nipple, her body releases Oxytocin (the labor hormone), causing gentle contractions.
We need 3 contractions in 10 minutes to judge the test.
Pay close attention here:
Negative = Normal (GOOD) 😊: The heart rate stays steady during contractions.
Positive = Abnormal (BAD) 😟: The heart rate dips late after the contraction starts (Late Deceleration). This means the baby is struggling.
After this test, mom has to stay for 30 minutes to make sure the contractions stop so she doesn't go into labor early!
Alright, class! Now that we've covered stress tests, let's talk about a very popular tool: Ultrasound! 🦇
Think of how a bat flies in the dark or a submarine sees underwater using sonar. That’s exactly what ultrasound is! It bounces sound waves off the baby to create a picture.
We can use this amazing tool to:
• Diagnose pregnancy as early as 6 weeks.
• Check the placenta and fluid.
• Check for defects (like heart or kidney issues).
• See the baby’s position (and maybe tell if it’s a boy or girl! 👶).
How does it work?
A device called a Transducer sends sound waves (too high for our ears to hear) into the uterus. The echoes bounce back and create an image on a screen.
We have two main "modes":
1. B-Mode (Gray-scale): This is like a black-and-white photo.
2. Real-time Mode: This is like a movie! 🎥 You can see the heart beating or the baby sucking their thumb.
Preparation is Key!
If mom is getting an abdominal ultrasound, we need her to have a FULL bladder. Why? Because a full bladder pushes the uterus up and acts like a window for the sound waves.
So, tell her to drink a full glass of water every 15 minutes starting 1.5 hours before the test, and don't pee! 🚽🚫
During the test, we put a towel under her right hip to prevent Supine Hypotension (so the heavy uterus doesn't squash her blood vessels). We use warm gel to help the transducer slide.
Now, let's look at... Biparietal Diameter (BPD).
This is just a fancy way of saying "measuring the baby's head from side to side." 📏 If the head is 8.5 cm wide, the baby usually weighs over 2.5kg and is likely around 40 weeks (ready to be born!).
We can also listen to the blood flow using Doppler Umbilical Velocimetry.
Imagine checking the speed of cars on a highway. 🚗💨 This test checks the speed of red blood cells in the vessels. It helps us see if blood is flowing easily or if there is resistance (which can happen if mom has diabetes or hypertension).
Another cool thing ultrasound does is Placental Grading.
Just like we get gray hair as we age, the placenta gets calcium deposits. We grade it from 0 (young) to 3 (mature). A Grade 3 placenta means the baby’s lungs are likely mature too!
Next up: Amniotic Fluid Volume. 💧
Think of the amniotic fluid as a swimming pool. If the baby is stressed, they pee less (yes, the fluid is partly baby pee!), so the pool level drops.
We measure "pockets" of fluid.
• Hydramnios: Too much fluid (Index > 20-24cm). Maybe the baby isn't swallowing?
• Oligohydramnios: Too little fluid (Index < 5-6cm). This is dangerous because the cord can get squashed!
Let's switch gears to blood tests: Maternal Serum Alpha-Fetoprotein (MSAFP).
Alpha-fetoprotein is made by the baby’s liver.
• If levels are HIGH in mom's blood: The baby might have an open defect (like Spina Bifida).
• If levels are LOW: It could suggest Down Syndrome.
To be more accurate, we do a Triple Screen (checking 3 things: AFP, Estriol, and hCG). This catches about 60-70% of Down Syndrome cases.
Now, let's talk about invasive tests (where we actually go inside). First is Chorionic Villi Sampling (CVS).
Done early (10-12 weeks), we take a tiny biopsy of the placenta "tree" (the villi) to check genetics.
Then we have the big one: Amniocentesis. 💉
This involves putting a needle through mom's belly into the uterus to take some fluid. We can do this as early as 12-13 weeks.
⚠️ Important Prep Difference: Unlike ultrasound, for Amniocentesis, we usually ask mom to EMPTY her bladder so we don't accidentally poke it!
What do we look for in the fluid?
1. Color: Should be like water. Yellow? Blood issue. Green? Meconium (baby pooped in distress).
2. L/S Ratio: This checks lung maturity. We want a ratio of 2:1. This means the lungs are strong enough to breathe air!
There is also Percutaneous Umbilical Blood Sampling (PUBS). This is where we take blood directly from the umbilical cord. It's great for checking anemia.
Or we can use a tiny camera called a Fetoscope to actually LOOK at the baby and even do surgery inside the womb! 😲 (Though this is rare and risky).
Finally, we put it all together in a Biophysical Profile (BPP). 📋
Think of this as the baby's "Report Card." We score the baby on 5 things (Movement, Tone, Breathing, Fluid, and Heart Rate).
• Score 8 to 10: Great job, baby is healthy! 🌟
• Score 4 to 6: Uh oh, baby might be in trouble.
